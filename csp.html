Complex steerable pyramids

Multiscale complex analysis, coefficient-space interventions, and their image-space interpretation.

This note specifies the representation underlying the CSP interventions in Steerable Lens. It distinguishes properties of the filter bank from properties of edited coefficients, and separates the local phase–displacement relation from claims about semantic or perceptual change.

Analysis and synthesis · Steerability · Phase and displacement · Interventions · References

1. Analysis and synthesis

Let x ∈ ℝH×W denote a single-channel image. Write the analysis operator as A and the synthesis operator as S. The coefficient collection contains oriented complex bands and two residuals:

A x = {rhi, {cs,k}s=0,…,J−1; k=0,…,K−1, rlo},   cs,k(u) ∈ ℂ. (1)

The analysis recursively separates a low-pass branch into oriented band-pass responses and a coarser low-pass branch. In a frequency-domain construction, radial and angular windows select spatial-frequency ranges and orientations; the recursive low-pass path supplies the scale dependence. The residuals preserve frequencies outside the oriented bands. The original architecture is described in [1].

For an appropriate analysis–synthesis pair, SAx reproduces x to the implementation’s reconstruction tolerance. This is not equivalent to ASc = c for arbitrary coefficient collections: the representation is redundant. Modified coefficients may lie outside the range of A. Consequently, preserving coefficient magnitudes before synthesis does not guarantee their preservation after synthesis, clipping, and reanalysis.

Tight-frame identities depend on normalization and sampling conventions. We do not assume S = A*, Parseval energy equality, or coefficient-space Euclidean distance equal to image-space distance for the experimental implementation. Frequency-domain boundary handling and discrete reconstruction accuracy also require explicit checks. See [4].

2. Orientation and complex coefficients

Steerability expresses an oriented filter response through a finite angular basis. For a steerable filter family:

ψθ = ∑k ak(θ) ψθk. (2)

The interpolation weights depend on the angular harmonics of that family. Steerability concerns orientation synthesis; it does not imply exact rotation invariance of a discretely sampled image representation. Nor should exact finite-basis steering be assumed for every analytic half-plane extension without checking its angular construction. [1, 2]

A complex band combines quadrature responses. At a position u, define:

cs,k(u) = as,k(u) + i bs,k(u) = ρs,k(u)eiφs,k(u),
ρ = √(a² + b²),   φ = atan2(b, a). (3)

Amplitude measures local response strength, while phase specifies alignment within the band’s oscillation. Phase is undefined at zero amplitude and poorly conditioned near zero. Quadrature energy and multiscale shiftability motivate this representation [2]; they do not identify phase with semantic content.

3. The local phase–displacement relation

Consider a narrow-band component with approximately constant local wavevector ω. Under the translation convention x′(u) = x(u − d), its phase change is approximately:

Δφ(u) ≈ −ωᵀd. (4)

This relation is exact for a plane wave and local for a spatially varying signal under suitable narrow-band and slowly varying envelope assumptions. It motivates phase-based motion processing [3]. A single orientation constrains only a projected displacement. Interpreting several phase changes as one displacement field requires cross-band compatibility.

Classifier gradients impose no such compatibility constraint. Independently edited phases can therefore produce texture changes, interference, or artifacts rather than a coherent warp. Large updates, phase wrapping, low-amplitude regions, and clipping further limit a geometric interpretation. Smooth-warp compatibility is measured after editing in this project; it is not a theorem about CSP phase edits.

4. Coefficient parameterization and induced image geometry

For the main MNIST and CelebA editors, let c⁰ = Ax₀ be the source coefficients. Real-valued fields δ and η parameterize phase and log-amplitude increments in the oriented bands:

cs,k(η,δ) = c⁰s,k exp(ηs,k + iδs,k),
x(η,δ) = clip[0,1](Re S{r⁰hi, c(η,δ), r⁰lo}). (5)

CSP phase fixes η = 0; Joint CSP optimizes both blocks. The residuals remain fixed in these editors. Multiplicative parameterization leaves an exactly zero source coefficient at zero. Log-amplitude changes are coefficient gains, not the spatial gain field used by the separate reconstruction model.

Writing the real parameter vector as q = (δ,η) and a differentiable driver objective as ℓ(x(q)), the chain rule gives:

∇qℓ = Jx(q)ᵀ ∇xℓ,   Jx(q) = ∂x/∂q.(6)

The coefficient derivatives are ∂c/∂δ = ic and ∂c/∂η = c. Where the rendering map is differentiable, its first-order image-distance geometry is:

‖x(q + Δq) − x(q)‖²₂ ≈ Δqᵀ Jx(q)ᵀJx(q) Δq. (7)

Thus identical parameter norms or amplification factors need not produce identical image changes. Our matched comparisons use rendered image-space L₂ distance. Equations (6–7) describe the parameterization’s local geometry, not a complete optimizer: the experimental routines additionally specify objective, preconditioning, parameter bounds, step selection, and budget attainment.

Recomputing the gradient in a fixed source parameterization is distinct from reanalyzing the current image at every step. The main editors do the former; the exploratory live natural-image paths use step-local updates. Frozen-source comparisons hold the initial direction fixed.
